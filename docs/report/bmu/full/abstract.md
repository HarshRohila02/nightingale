Acute chest pain is a frequent reason for emergency visits, and its dangerous causes can look like benign ones. We built Nightingale, a research prototype that supports a clinician by ranking 14 possible causes of acute chest pain. It never gives a diagnosis. A small transformer trained on synthetic DDXPlus patients ranks the causes, a cardiac knowledge graph scores them separately, and a logarithmic opinion pool fuses the two. Independent red-flag rules, written after published clinical patterns, list the dangerous causes first. Each ranking is explained from the graph and the model's own reasons. Evidence passages are shown beside the top candidates and never used to rank.

We pre-registered the evaluation, scored every system at 100%, 50% and 25% of each history, froze the code, and opened the test split once (34,657 patients). Six hypotheses are not supported: fusion did not beat its machine-learning component (H1, H1-R); fusion with red flags did not improve safety (H2, H2-R); grounding did not lower unsupported claims (H3; the language-model explainer failed its checkpoint and does not ship); and the transformer did not beat every comparator trained like it (H6). H4, H5 and the Minimum hold, but H4's support is hollow: the knowledge graph adds nothing measurable; the contrast comes from the red flags reordering the list.

The result that carries concerns safety when answers are missing. At 25% of the history, must-not-miss recall@3 is 0.8710 [0.8659, 0.8764] for the original XGBoost model (B1-XGB, `[TEST]`) and 0.9198 [0.9156, 0.9238] for the original logistic regression (B1-LR, `[TEST]`), both under the 0.95 target. Every model trained on masked copies of the histories meets it, for example 0.9986 [0.9980, 0.9991] (A0′, `[TEST]`).

The data are synthetic, the system knows 14 conditions only, random masks are not history-taking, and no clinician validated any output.
